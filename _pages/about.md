I am Shi-Jie Gao (高世杰), a postdoctoral researcher at the School of Astronomy and Space Science, Nanjing University.

My research focuses on compact objects and their binary evolution, especially neutron stars, black holes, X-ray binaries, pulsars, and radio transients.

I work at the intersection of theoretical modeling and observational data analysis. On the theory side, I study how compact binaries form and evolve, including X-ray binaries, binary pulsars, and possible formation channels for mass-gap black holes. On the observational side, I search for and characterize radio pulsars and transients using data from facilities such as FAST and the Green Bank Telescope.

I am also involved in observing practice and outreach with the 4.5-m radio telescope at Nanjing University's [Zuo Dijiang (左涤江) Observatory](/zuo-dijiang-observatory/). One focus of this work is to show how a small radio telescope can receive pulsar signals, from antenna pointing and data acquisition to signal processing and interpretation.

Research Interests
======

* Compact objects: neutron stars, black holes, and white dwarfs
* Binary evolution and X-ray binaries
* Radio pulsars, millisecond pulsars, and rotating radio transients


Selected Highlights
======

* Reported the discovery of 19 pulsars in FAST archival data in [*Pulsar Gleaners*](https://astro-sjgao.github.io/PulsarGleaners/), a title chosen to describe recovering overlooked pulsars from archival observations.
* Discovered the first radio pulsar associated with the globular cluster Terzan 6, PSR J1750-3116A, using high-frequency Green Bank Telescope data.
* Studied the formation of mass-gap black holes from neutron-star X-ray binaries with super-Eddington accretion.
* Help develop observing and outreach material for Nanjing University's 4.5-m radio telescope, including a presentation on receiving pulsar signals.

Background
======

Before joining Nanjing University, I studied physics at Shandong University. My undergraduate training in physics led me toward astronomy and high-energy astrophysics, where compact objects provide a natural laboratory for gravity, plasma physics, stellar evolution, and extreme radiation processes.

I am always interested in questions that connect physical modeling with real astronomical data: how compact binaries form, how pulsars reveal themselves in noisy observations, and what these systems can tell us about the life cycles of stars.
